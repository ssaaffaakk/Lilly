# Is the diacritic-exact gate defensible? What the literature says

Researched 2026-09-02, five questions, web sources fetched and cited. Prompted
by the gate refusing pass-14 and pass-15, and by the question of whether
requiring č ć đ š ž in an exact string match is normal practice or a constraint
this project invented for itself.

Short answer: **stricter than the norm for this model family**, and the
diacritics do not need to come from the recogniser at all.

## 1. Diacritic-blind evaluation

*Confidence: high*

There is no field-wide standard requiring diacritics in OCR/ASR scoring — normalization is treated as an explicitly declared, per-benchmark policy choice, and "ignore diacritics" is a first-class option in mainstream OCR evaluation tooling (ocrevalUAtion, from the IMPACT project). The document-OCR world (OCR-D, ISRI/ocreval) defaults to diacritic-sensitive scoring but normalizes only Unicode encoding (NFC canonical equivalence), not diacritics themselves; a peer-reviewed survey of these tools concludes CER/WER cannot even be compared across implementations because they normalize differently. The scene-text recognition family that EasyOCR belongs to goes much further in the opposite direction: the standard benchmark protocol (Baek et al., ICCV 2019) scores on a 36-character case-insensitive alphanumeric label set with non-alphanumeric images filtered out, so accented characters are not in the label space at all, and ICDAR's RRC-MLT-2019 end-to-end task matches case-insensitively and marks any word containing a character unseen in training as "don't care," excluded from scoring. On the other side, the closest South Slavic benchmark (ParlaSpeech-HR) computes CER/WER on full Croatian orthography with č/ć/đ/š/ž intact, and diacritic-fold-then-restore is treated as a legitimate separate task for exactly these languages. So a strict exact-match-with-diacritics gate is one defensible convention among several, not the universal norm — but I found no shared task or benchmark for Bosnian/Croatian/Serbian OCR that reports a diacritic-insensitive metric, so a project adopting one would be making a documented but locally novel choice rather than citing precedent.

## 2. Diacritic restoration as a separate step

*Confidence: medium*

Yes — diacritic restoration is a well-established standalone NLP task with its own literature, benchmarks and off-the-shelf tools, and it is routinely done as a separate step on text that has already been recognised or typed without diacritics. For Croatian/Serbian/Slovene specifically, Ljubešić et al. (LREC 2016) reach 99.57% token accuracy on standard Croatian text and 99.38% on non-standard (Twitter) text, and released the system as `redi`, still the diacritic-restoration service offered by CLARIN.SI; a BERT tagger (Náplava et al., PBML 2021) reaches 99.73% alpha-word accuracy on Croatian, 99.22% Czech, 99.41% Hungarian, 98.95% Turkish, 98.64% Romanian, 98.53% Vietnamese. Even the lexicon-only variant (most-frequent diacritised form, no language model, ~1 GB) hits 99.36% on Croatian, so the cheap version is nearly as good as the expensive one. CRITICAL CAVEAT: every one of these numbers is measured on *cleanly and systematically de-diacritised* text (diacritics stripped programmatically from correct text), not on noisy OCR output — I found no study that measures restoration accuracy on OCR hypotheses, and no head-to-head experiment comparing "recogniser emits diacritics" against "recogniser drops them, restorer puts them back". The residual errors are also concentrated exactly where a sign-translator lives: proper nouns unseen in training (30% of errors on standard text) and genuinely ambiguous words (21–37%).

## 3. What missing diacritics cost the translation

*Confidence: medium*

The published evidence points mostly toward robustness, and the strongest single study is directly on-point for the direction that matters here (X→English). Chen et al. (NAACL 2024), covering 55 languages including Slovenian, found that stripping source-side diacritics moves BLEU by essentially zero — group differences bounded within +0.66 to −0.78 BLEU, Cohen's d of 0.08–0.17 even where p<0.05 — and they attribute this to context supplying what the diacritic would have. Adelani et al. (2021) found the same asymmetry concretely: into English, undiacritized Yorùbá source scored 14.0 vs 14.0 BLEU (and 13.9 undiacritized vs 13.2 diacritized for the fine-tuned system) once in-domain data was present, while the reverse direction lost heavily. Sennrich et al. (WMT16) went further and deliberately removed Romanian source diacritics for RO→EN, gaining +1.3–1.4 BLEU, because diacritics were inconsistent in the data — the same inconsistency an OCR reader produces. Against this, Belinkov & Bisk (ICLR 2018) show catastrophic drops on "natural noise" whose bulk (in French) is diacritic errors — FR→EN 42.54→17.42 BLEU — but that noise is a mixture applied to every replaceable word under a train-clean/test-noisy mismatch, so it is an upper bound on damage rather than a measurement of diacritics alone. For BCS specifically: ~13% of tokens in running Croatian/Serbian/Slovene text carry diacritics (Ljubešić et al. 2016), but only ~5% of de-diacriticised Serbian word types are genuinely ambiguous — 95% of 943,804 folded forms have exactly one candidate (Krstev et al. 2018) — so folding touches many tokens while creating real ambiguity in few. No study I could find measures BCS→English MT quality with diacritics stripped from the source, and none uses chrF or COMET for this question.

### Not found

Four things I could not find, stated plainly. (1) No study measures Bosnian/Croatian/Serbian→English MT quality with diacritics stripped from the source. The nearest data point is Slovenian inside the 19-language European set of Chen et al. 2024, and that paper reports aggregate curves and group means rather than a per-language Slovenian number in the text I could read. Everything BCS-specific I found (Ljubešić et al. 2016, Krstev et al. 2018) measures diacritic RESTORATION accuracy, never downstream translation. (2) No chrF or COMET measurement of diacritic folding exists in anything I fetched — every number in this report is BLEU, and Chen et al. explicitly use SacreBLEU only. Since chrF is character-based, a folded diacritic costs it less than it costs an exact-match gate but more than it costs BLEU; nobody has measured that for this question. (3) No study covers short, contextless input. The mechanism both robustness papers credit — neighbouring words disambiguate the folded form — is precisely what a one-to-three-word shop sign does not supply. ## 4. Why training on our own output failed

*Confidence: high*

Your failure mode is the single best-documented failure mode in the self-training literature, and the specific shape of it — a rare feature vanishing from the pseudo-labels and then from the model — is named in three separate literatures. (1) Confirmation bias: the network overfits its own erroneous predictions and, because overconfidence is a consequence of hard-label training, high confidence is not evidence of correctness (Arazo et al.; and for text recognition specifically, Seq-UPS states that "erroneous high confidence pseudo-labels generated from poorly calibrated models" render "threshold-based selection ineffective"). (2) Rare-feature loss: model collapse literature says the tails of the distribution disappear first (Shumailov et al., Nature 2024), and a 2026 theory preprint formalizes the same thing as "signal forgetting" — self-training acts as a spectral filter where weak features "are rapidly forgotten, resembling the isotropic noise," while strong ones survive. Your 10.6% → 1% diacritic rate is exactly a tail being filtered out before training, and the model then learned the filtered distribution. (3) Confidence thresholding as the cause rather than the cure: your conf ≥ 0.85 cut is a class-unaware filter, and Zou et al. (ECCV 2018) show such filtering is "intrinsically biased towards the easy (i.e., more confident) classes"; Kahn et al. (ICASSP 2020) measured the trade-off directly and found "more aggressive filtering improves the label quality but results in worse model performance"; Wolf & Fink's handwriting self-training "diverges for thresholds greater 0.55." Notably, Noisy Student — the canonical success — used a confidence threshold of 0.3, not 0.85, and explicitly balanced classes by duplicating under-represented ones. Self-training does help, including in OCR: Wolf & Fink cut CER from 25.41% to 12.37% training only on pseudo-labels, and Rijhwani et al. (TACL 2021) got 15–29% relative error reduction on endangered-language OCR — but only, they say, when self-training is combined with lexically aware decoding. The standard fixes are all documented: class/rare-feature-balanced selection instead of a global threshold, a lexicon or count-based LM constraining decoding, ensembles or a different/adapted teacher rather than the model labelling itself, keeping real human-labelled data in every training mixture, and early stopping. I found no study measuring diacritic-specific degradation in OCR self-training — that exact result appears to be undocumented.

## 5. Cyrillic

*Confidence: high*

Dropping the Cyrillic cities from *training* was right for the reason you had — the labels were invented text — but discarding Cyrillic signs from the *product* is not what the literature does. Script identification before recognition is standard, documented practice in multi-script OCR: the ICDAR MLT competitions run script classification as a dedicated task, Gomez et al. state plainly that "text recognition algorithms are language-dependent" so detecting the script "allows selecting the correct language model," and Huang et al. (CVPR 2021) route each detected word to a script-specific recognition head via a Language Prediction Network. Google's Fujii et al. (ICDAR 2017) quantify the stake: better script ID cut "the character error rate attributable to script misidentification by 33%," which confirms wrong-script routing is a measured, named error class rather than an edge case. Off-the-shelf Cyrillic recognisers exist and are trivially available — EasyOCR ships `cyrillic_g2` with `rs_cyrillic` in its language list, PaddleOCR lists both `rs_cyrillic` and `rs_latin`, and Tesseract ships `srp`, `srp_latn` and a script-level `Cyrillic` model — and EasyOCR's own source refuses to mix script families in one Reader (a `ValueError`), so a Latin-only reader is architecturally incapable of Cyrillic, not merely inaccurate on it. Crucially the transliteration direction you need is the safe one: Cyrillic→Latin for Serbian is unambiguous because Cyrillic uses no digraphs, while Latin→Cyrillic needs language knowledge (nadživeti vs. nadžak, injekcija) — and transliterating everything to Latin is exactly what mainstream South Slavic NLP does (BERTić's 8-billion-token collection: "All texts were transliterated to the Latin script").
